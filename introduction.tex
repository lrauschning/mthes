Recent progress in genome sequencing and assembly technologies have made full genome assemblies more and more accessible, to the point where it is now feasible to obtain assemblies for large sample populations in many species\cite{guo25, avni26}.
Translating this data into knowledge has, however, been a significant challenge so far.
Genome assemblies are a large and heterogeneous kind of data.
Making the information contained within them accessible is far from trivial, and requires carefully designed computational tools to efficiently handle large datasets and work well in HPC systems.

The matter of how to represent today's genome assembly datasets is absolutely crucial to any attempt at leveraging today's plentyful data to gain understanding.
A well-designed representation is required to design efficient algorithms, but any representation will also limit the questions we can ask a dataset.%nlimiting, enabling or focusing the questions that we ask a dataset.
Despite several recent proposals and a slowly maturing software ecosystem\cite{gfatools,garrison18}, I will argue that the representation of today's genome datasets is an unsolved question still requiring considerable conceptual contributions.

\section*{Historical Background to Modern Genomics}

The first recognition of the genome as a concept dates to Gregor Mendels work in peas\cite{mendel66, portin17}.
He represented the genome of his peas as combinations of upper/lowercase letters indicating different alleles.
Given the lack of any genomic technologies, the genetic identity of a given pea could only be determined by its phenotype or the phenotypes of its offspring in a controlled cross.
Mendel's genomes also lacked any internal structure; gametes were viewed as produced from independently assorted alleles of the parental genotypes.
The genome as such was a passive container of alleles, and not conceptualized beyond this.\\
After the rediscovery of Mendel's work in the early 20th century, the discovery of chromosomes as the bearer of heredity information and further crossing experiments performed among others by Thomas Hunt Morgan's laboratory in \textit{Drosophila melanogaster} revealed the genome to consist of chromosomes with a linear structure\cite{sturtevant13, portin17}.
Through careful crossing, chromosomes were mapped, again determining the genotype solely from the phenotype of the organism and its offspring.
These genetic maps charted genes with observable phenotypes along the chromosomes, and revealed a shuffling process known as recombination, acting by discrete crossover events with a determinable likelihood at each position along the genetic map.\\
The genome had evolved from a few unordered letters to a set of linear maps, whose cellular component (if not molecular identity) was known to contemporary researchers.
Like Mendel, however, genes along the genome were thought of and named for their observable, segregating phenotypes, and the absence of any genomic technologies made genotyping a slow and labourious process.\\
Later, microscopy of chromosomes revealed a banded structure, which work in maize by Barbara McClintock and in \textit{Drosophila spp.} by Theodosius Dobzhansky showed to sometimes vary across individuals, initially without giving rise to observable macroscopic phenotypes\cite{dobzhansky29}.
The discovery of these so-called »cryptic polymorphisms« was the first time a genotype had been determined by other means than phenotypic observation.
Cytogenetics can thus be termed the first genomic technology; in fact, through careful observation of the band structure, these polymorphisms could be identified as inverted or copy-pasted segments along the chromosomes, hinting at the mechanism of their formation -- later giving rise to McClintock's discovery of transposable elements\cite{mcclintock50}.\\

%Applying this new population genetic theory to the observed allele frequency of inversions in captive \textit{D. pseudoobscura} populations, these »cryptic polymorphisms« were revealed to have an effect on observed fitness, in particular in heterozygotes, a phenomenon that was then termed heterosis.

Increasingly, the study of the genome became emancipated from the reliance of clearly segregating phenotypes for every genetic polymorphism, and the discovery of rearrangements revealed that genomes in a species could vary beyond the single-gene level. % a genetic or cytogenetic map could vary between different individuals of a population.
%Genotyping technology allowed population genetic studies for the first time, though these were limited in the number of polymorphisms that could be studied at a time. %studied a limited amount of polymorphisms at a time.

The discovery first of DNA as the molecule carrying genetic information and later of its 3D structure in the 1940s and 1950s by Avery\cite{avery42} and Franklin, Crick and Watson\cite{watson53} provided for the first time a vision to describe the genome at a molecular level.
More molecular studies followed like the decoding of the genetic code and the discovery of promoters and transcription factor binding sites, providing some understanding on a basepair level\cite{nirenberg61, roeder19}.
This opened the door to a radically different way of conceptualizing the genome -- not as an ordered map of segregating phenotypes or bands under a microscope, but as a string of bases along a phosphate backbone.
As DNA became accessible by cloning, PCR and eventually early sequencing methods, databases of DNA sequences started to emerge.
These would contain small fragments of the genome, often genes of particular interest, promotor or plasmid sequences.
The sequences would be stored as letters, but unlike Mendel's early use of letters to indicate alleles, the letters now indicated the bases \texttt{A}denine, \texttt{C}ytosine, \texttt{G}uanine and \texttt{T}hymine and were ordered by their position in the genome, typically starting from the $5'$ and ending at the $3'$ end. 
Early databases were still printed on paper and distributed by mail as books to university libraries, but they soon embraced early mainframe computers as they became available to universities.
Databases used a variety of file formats at the time -- often based on previous print conventions --, but the format used by the popular FASTP\cite{lipman85} and FASTA\cite{pearson88} programs came to dominate. %, but for nucleic acid databases the format supported by the fast-All programme, colloquially known as FASTA format, soon took the lead.
This format represents sequence in an \texttt{ACGT}-encoding with nonsignificant linebreaks to fit on early computer screens, along with header lines containing the sequence identifier to differentiate different sequences in the same file. 
Additional metadata can also be added to the header line, and gaps inserted indicating individual unknown bases or unresolved breaks in the sequence. %for each sequence containing the sequence name and additional metadata, if present. 
FASTA's format and variants based on it are still in common use today for a variety of purposes. 
In particular, it has become the de facto standard for representing reference genome assemblies after being chosen by major reference genome projects for distributing their references.%, and FASTQ format

Increasing throughput of Sanger sequencing opened up the possibility of obtaining the base sequence of entire genomes.
After early success in viral and bacterial genomes in the early 1990s, this culminated in large, well-funded global consortia preparing reference genomes for a number of eukaryotic species in the late 90s and early 00s, most notably \textit{A. thaliana}, mouse and human\cite{athal00, chinwalla02, lander01, venter01}.
% revive genetic map
The ambition to sequence not just individual genes of interest, but the entire genome can be viewed as a revival of Hunt Morgan's concept of a per-species genetic map.
The genome would not just be a scientific outcome in and of itself, but serve also as a community resource and to avoid duplicating sequencing and mapping efforts by individual groups.
A major component of specifically the Human Genome Project was also technology and standards development, along with outreach to the scientific community and general public.
Altogether, this wave of genome projects aimed to establish a new mode of thinking about genomes and more broadly biology, »ushering in the post-genomic era« in the language of the time\cite{perbal15, collins03}.
% gaps as novel concept, contiguity metrics

With the reference genome deciphered for some common model species, genomicists were now able to compare samples to the reference genome -- both conceptually, and by using the reference to develop cost-efficient microarrays.
This powered the HapMap project immediately after the human reference had been assembled, aided by the long linkage blocks and low overall variation in the human genome\cite{altshuler05}.
% short read sequencing useful + cheap
Later, sequencing costs declined sharply with the advent of early next-generation sequencing technologies.
Especially in the beginning, these produced very short and error-prone untargeted reads that were not directly useful on their own.
However, the reads could be aligned to the reference genome, recovering the position they were sampled from.
Many classic read alignment tools were developed in this period, based on earlier approaches for searching for genes in reference genomes and databases\cite{li08, kent02}.
Small differences in sequence between sample and reference could then be determined from these aligned reads using statistical models\cite{li08, mckenna10}.
A key advantage over microarrays was the ability for \textit{de novo} variant discovery and to avoid the need for labourious microarray design and manufacturing, particularly in non-model organisms.
At least for single-nucleotide variants or small indels in non-repetitive regions, next-generation sequencing allowed scalable comprehensive genotyping.
This drove a wave of projects sequencing ever larger samples, such as the 1000 Genome Project in human and the 1001 Genomes Project in \textit{A. thaliana}\cite{weigel09, durbin10}.
The motivation for these projects was increasingly shifting away from a technology development and community resource perspective towards association studies and medical applications (in humans).

In order to efficiently compute associations between genotype and phenotype, genotypes of multiple samples have to be aggregated into a single, numerical matrix representation\cite{shabalin12}.
To store the genotypes collected in large-scale sequencing efforts, the Variant Call Format was developed by the 1000 Genomes Project\cite{durbin10}.
VCF closely follows this matrix representation, and while updates over the years have added ways to encode larger variations into the standard, its representation remains centred on cataloguing single-nucleotide polymorphisms in multiple samples\cite{htsspecs}.\\
This represented a step away from genome assembly, going from the deciphering of the exact (if usually not complete) base sequence to a list of genotypes at segregating sites, analogous to Mendel's genotypes but at much higher scale and without requiring directly observable phenotypes.

\section*{Assembling Genomes}

In this section, I will give a brief introduction into the process used to assemble a genome, highlighting the current state of the art.
In particular, I will focus on long read-based \textit{de novo} phased assembly, as this offers the most complete characterization of the genome and has recently become economically possible in larger samples, including in species with fairly complex genomes.

\subsection*{DNA Sequencing Requirements}

% assembly now within reach
% go into assembly algos
As genomes are far too large to be sequenced in one go, they are fragmented and the base sequence of some of these fragments reconstructed; these sequenced fragments are known as reads.
The problem of reconstructing the sequence of the entire genome from the sequence of the sampled reads is known as genome assembly, and is often compared to assembling a jigsaw puzzle from its pieces.
However, there are a few key differences between genome assembly and solving a puzzle; chiefly, sequencing reads vary in length and may contain sequencing errors or PCR artifacts, and genomes often contain highly repetitive regions.
As an additional difference, reads are typically sampled randomly from the genome, an approach known for historical reasons as shotgun sequencing\cite{anderson81, green97}.
Unlike the regular coverage of a picture by its jigsaw pieces, this requires accounting for significant stochasticity in the input dataset.\\
We can already see a minimum requirement for a dataset of sequenced reads to be used to successfully assemble a genome; there need to be sufficient reads at every position to correct any sequencing errors present in individual reads, lest they be propagated to the final genome assembly.
As during sequencing certain motifs are far more error prone than others, this means substantially more sequencing may be required than is sufficient to just cover every position once on average. %error rates are not distributed evenly, this  
In practice, this is usually a minor problem, as it can be avoided by simply sequencing more -- but read errors add significant complexity to genome assembly techniques, as explained in the next subsection.\\
%Both of these problems can be avoided by sampling more reads, so are in practice a minor problem in datasets of sufficient size -- but they still need to be accounted for during genome assembly.\\
A more stubborn problem is presented by repetitive or low-complexity regions.
These make reconstruction challenging by causing spurious similarity between reads, obscuring the real similarity signal -- in the puzzle analogy, this is analogous to the blue sky or dense foliage being the most challenging regions in solving a jigsaw puzzle.\\
Longer reads can help resolve repetitive sequences in two ways.
First, a sufficiently long read can span the entire region containing the repeat, allowing direct reconstruction.
Second, most repetitive regions are not exact repeats; like other regions in the genome, they accumulate point mutations as they evolve.

\subsection*{Long Read Sequencing}

As outlined in the Historical Background section, modern genome assembly relies on chiefly on long reads, typically defined as sequencing technologies allowing fragments of at least \qty{10}{\kilo\bp} to be assembled.
In contrast, earlier short read approaches could sequence no more than about \qty{400}{\bp}, or about \qty{1}{\kilo\bp} when considering paired ends of a read with an unsequenced gap in between.\\
Today, two companies dominate long read sequencing: Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT), founded in 2005, and Pacific Biosciences (PacBio), founded as Nanofluidics Inc. in 2004.
The two companies develop two completely different sequencing technologies:
PacBio's approach is based on sequencing by synthesis, similar to dominant short read techologies.
%A single DNA polymerase is attached to the bottom of a Zero-Mode Waveguide engineered on a lithographically produced flowcell.
The sample DNA is introduced in single-stranded state along with labelled nucleotides.
As the polymerase synthesizes the complementary strand to the input DNA, the label is cleaved from the incorporated nucleotide.
This results in a fluorescence signal% in the Zero-Mode Waveguide
, which is read out by the sequencer to reconstruct the read's base sequence.
This sequencing technology is sometimes also known as Single-Molecule, Real-Time (SMRT) Sequencing\cite{pbtech}.\\
In contrast, ONT's platform relies on a nanopore, as implied by the name.
The biotechnologically produced nanopore is situated in a membrane separating two small cells, across which a voltage is applied.
This induces an ionic current that flows through the pore, and scales with the pore's resistance and the applied voltage\cite{onttech}.
The sample DNA is attached to a motor protein, and fed through the pore.
The resistance of the pore and thus the measured current varies based on which nucleotides are currently in the narrow pore.
From this signal, the read sequence is reconstructed using a Machine Learning model\cite{onttech, wang21}.

Modern developments in long-read sequencing technology arguably started with PacBio's announcement of HiFi sequencing in 2018, providing for the first time long reads with a low error rate\cite{pbhifi}.
This as made possible by preparing a sequencing library containing circular instead of linear molecules of DNA with an adapter sequence, allowing the same read to be sequenced multiple times and a consensus call to be made.\\
In 2023, PacBio released the Revio sequencing platform, drastically improving throughput and driving down sequencing costs both on release and in subsequent improvements to the platform\cite{pbrevio25, pbrevio23}.
%This increased competition also helped drive down prices for ONT sequencing.%, and alltogether contributed to making long reads the new standard in genome assembly.

At the same time, ONT's sequencing platform received multiple improvements, helping to reduce its error rate and increase throughput\cite{wang21}.
Most notable among these are the release of the R10 pore chemistry in 2020 and the Dorado basecaller in 2022\cite{doradorelease, r10.3release, sanderson23}.
% HERRO here?
In 2024, HERRO was released, demonstrating a substantial reduction in error rate for ONT reads by all-against-all alignment and Machine Learning-aided consensus calling of varying bases\cite{stanojevic24}.
This technique was quickly adopted both by ONT's Dorado basecaller (there called Dorado correct) and hifiasm.

While Oxford Nanopore Technology's sequencing platform had always been capable of sequencing very long reads\cite{jain18}, more stable pores and library preparation protocols optimized for ultralong reads made longer and longer read lengths achievable in practice.
%referred to as ultralong, %focused continuous developments in library preparation but suffered from higher error rates as well as throughput.
%While traditional PacBio or ONT long reads are usually shorter than \qty{30}{\kilo\bp}, the development of ultralong library preparation protocols have made it possible to sequence longer and longer reads with ONT.
Individual megabase-scale reads are now regularly observed in mammalian genomes, with the longest reported read announced by Oxford Nanopore to be \qty{4.3}{\mega\bp}, or more than twice the size of the \textit{Haemophilus influenzae} genome, the first non-viral genome ever assembled\cite{whales, fleischmann95}.

To summarize, both ONT and PacBio sell state-of-the-art long read sequencing platforms.
While ONT can produce dramatically longer reads with the right library preparation, PacBio still has a lower error rate, though this gap has gotten much narrower with recent developments.
While PacBio sequencing machines are bulky and expensive, ONT sequencers are small and more affordable, though costs for consumables are still higher and throughput is lower per machine.

\subsection*{Assembly Algorithms}

% need connection here
Sequencing a genome is is not enough to obtain a genomic sequence; the genome assembly puzzle still needs to be solved.
Even for the smallest eukaryotic genomes, sequencing datasets are far too large for humans to process, making computer programs the mainstay for genome assembly from the very beginning, though human judgement is still often required for quality control, setting appropriate parameters and scaffolding.
Genome assembly is a complex computational problem
% footnote or main text?
\footnote{Most formulations fall within the complexity class of $\mathcal{NP}$-complete problems, with the notable exception of certain Eulerian path approaches that are polynomially bounded by the number of k-mers in a genome\cite{pop02, compeau11}.},
meaning that heuristic algorithms are required to make the problem tractable.
Early approaches used simple greedy heuristics joining maximally overlapping reads, but these often caused misjoins between different sequences containing the same transposable elements\cite{huang92, huang99, green96}.
Almost all later assembly softwares use one of two algorithmic paradigms, both based on a graph representation of the sequencing reads
\footnote{One recently developed linear-time algorithm presents a notable exception, see \cite{wong23}}.\\
De Bruijn-graph based assemblers split reads into overlapping fragments, termed $k$-mers.
A $k-mer$ found in the reads is represented in the de Bruijn graph as an edge.
If they are not already in the graph, the two $k$-mers of length $k-1$ obtained by removing the first and last letter from the original $k$-mer are added to the graph as (labelled) nodes.
They are connected by an edge corresponding to the read $k$-mer\cite{pevzner01, compeau11}.
The genome has to contain all $k$-mers found in the reads; as such, it corresponds to a path traversing all edges in the graph, also known as an Eulerian path.
The Eulerian path in a graph can be found efficiently, but exact repeats of size greater than $k$ will be collapsed, and sequencing errors need to be identified and corrected prior to graph construction.
Most de Bruijn-graph based assemblers include additional heuristics to address these limitations.\\
The other approach is referred to as Overlap-Layout-Consensus (OLC)\cite{kececioglu95}.
In the graph representation used, entire reads are represented as nodes.
An edge is added between reads with overlapping sequence at their ends -- this is known as the overlap step.
This initial representation, also referred to as an overlap graph, is processed in a variety of ways, notably by compressing unbranching paths, removing transitive edges and reads entirely contained within another read.
From this simplified graph representation, known as a contig or assembly graph, the genome is reconstructed in the layout step by searching for an optimal path covering all nodes (equivalent to finding a linear layout of the graph, hence the name).
Finally, reads are aligned back to the path to correct sequencing errors in the final consensus step.
Particularly the layout step is a very hard algorithmic problem, requiring aggressive processing of the graph representation and heuristic approaches in order to be tractable.
Finding all read overlaps is also a computationally demanding step, although it can be parallelized efficiently.

Remarkably, both of these approaches have been adjusted to successive generations of sequencing technologies over the years, and neither has proven to definitely outperform the other as of now.\\
Currently, two assemblers are used for state of the art assembly.
Verkko is based on a de Bruijn graph approach, and hifiasm uses the OLC paradigm\cite{rautiainen23, cheng21}.
Both of these assemblers have similar specialized procedures integrating ONT ultralong reads to resolve difficult regions and improve assembly contiguity, as well as preprocessing steps performing read error correction\cite{cheng24, antipov25}.
To integrate ultralong reads, they are aligned to the initial unitig graph(hifiasm)/a compressed de Bruijn graph (verkko).
These alignments are then used to refine the graph, by adding additional edges (both) and duplicating nodes to resolve ambiguous paths (only hifiasm).
This allows both assemblers to utilize ultralong ONT reads to similar effect, boosting contiguity and resolving repetitive regions inaccessible with shorter reads.
Though hifiasm tends to be faster and easier to use as a single command-line tool compared to verkkos architecture as a pipeline chaining many steps, both assemblers produce assemblies of similar quality.
Though they differ in their underlying algorithmic paradigm, this is not too surprising given that recent performance improvements have largely come from the integration of ultralong ONT reads and read error correction before assembly, steps that both assemblers implement in a similar manner.

However, most eukaryotes do not have a single genome to reconstruct; cells often carry multiple copies of the genome, referred to as haplotypes.

\textit{A. thaliana} has two copies of its genome, following the definition of a diploid.
However, due to its generally selfing nature, in most samples not derived from a deliberate cross, these two copies will be almost identical save for individual \textit{de novo} mutations.
For this reason, \textit{A. thaliana} is often referred to as a haplodiploid (rhyming haploid, the term for organisms carrying only a single copy of their genome).
Humans and many other animals are also diploids, but generally have distinct haplotypes derived from their parents.\\ %different but compatible copies of their genome 
Variation on the same haplotype is inherited together, unless recombination breaks it up; this has given haplotypes the moniker »the unit of heredity«.
As recombination switches genomic sequence between different haplotypes in the same organism, it provides an evolutionary force preventing different haplotypes of a population from becoming too divergent from another\cite{renkawitz14, penalba20}.%or incompatible with each other if they occur within the same population
\\

Separately assembling the different haplotypes of a sample is known as phased assembly.
This is a formidable challenge, compounded by the ploidy of the sample and the length of regions where different haplotypes share the same sequence -- unfortunately, both high ploidy and long identical stretches are common in crops as a result of (in-)breeding and bottlenecks associated with domestication.
While in allopolyploids, different subgenomes will generally have divergent haplotypes, in autopolyploids recombination can break these up, making them more challenging to phase.%\cite{sun25}.%In autopolyploids, recombination acts between all haplotypes, % causing their gradual assimilation \cite{sun25}
\\
Traditionally, separating different haplotypes during assembly has required information beyond that present in the reads.
% additional info, highlight hic in particular
A common approach known as trio sequencing requires sequencing the parents of the sample, using variants unique to one parent to infer the haplotype of reads or contigs.
A similar approach sequences the offspring or, as recently demonstrated, gametes of an individual\cite{campoy20}.
Trio sequencing requires the parents of the sample to be known and accessible, and all of these approaches except gamete sequencing require additional samples to be grown, besides the need for more sequencing.
These approaches also get more complicated the more copies of the genome an organism has: in trio sequencing, one would need to go back one additional generation for every doubling in ploidy, but every generation adds additional recombination events complicating inference in the sample's data.\\ %and additionally recombination events in the family tree leading from the sampled ancestors 
Both of these approaches rely on haplotypes, as units of heredity, being generally inherited together.
This effect is also visible in a population, and indeed a haplotype panel derived from a population matching the sample can be used to infer its phasing.
As this leverages more indirect relations than that between parent and offspring, it is more likely to miss recombination events, and generally produces short-range phasing information not considered sufficient for high-quality phased assembly.
\\
Proximity ligation data can also be used to infer the phasing of haplotypes, and does not require access to additional samples.
This is the approach recommended by the Earth Biogenome Project, though the most common HiC- or OmniC-platforms require additional library and short-read sequencing for every sample\cite{ebpseqrecs}.
The short reads used by most proximity ligation platforms may not uniquely align to a haplotype, particularly in highly polyploid and repetitive genomes.
The recent development of long-read proximity ligation assays may help to address this as they become more widely available.

Ideally, one would phase an assembly directly using the sequencing reads; this has been demonstrated in humans by the hifiasm team, using ONT ultralong reads and an assembly algorithm especially designed for using these reads in phasing (now part of hifiasm)\cite{cheng21}.

\subsection*{Genome Assembly in 2026}
As outlined above, the last few years have seen major developments in DNA sequencing technology, and in the algorithms tthat use them to assemble genomes.  
It is now routinely possible to produce chromosome-scale assemblies -- that is, assemblies that are largely complete and contiguous\cite{ebpseqrecs, sarashetti24}.
In diploids, these assemblies can usually be phased to a high standard, although phasing in polyploids is still less reliable and often requires manual curation.

As the error rates of ONT sequencing have gotten lower, it has been demonstrated that genomes can be assembled using just their sequencing technology\cite{ebpseqrecs, cheng26}. % TODO more citations
In fact, one recent preprint demonstrated chromosome-scale phased assembly in humans (as well as long-read based contact map profiling) using only 4 ONT flowcells at an estimated cost of approximately 3000€ per sample \cite{gross26}.
Using only a single platform not only saves costs, but also simplifies planning and library preparation, allowing smaller teams to process more samples.
While for humans and other diploids, experience has provided a »recipe« for a high-quality phased assembly that works reliably, for polyploids the process is still more ad hoc, with unclear input requirements and manual curation\cite{ebseq}.
One of the aims of this thesis is to derive a lower bound for  input requirement for phasing an autotetraploid potato genome using only ONT long reads, like is now state of the art in humans.
It is presented in the first part of the Main chapter.

As significant effort is spent assembling complete genomes, it is desirable to use a representation that can adequately represent all of the genome.
All major eukaryotic pangenome projects have therefore focused on sequence-based pangenome representations, which will also form the basis of this thesis.
Almost all sequence-oriented pangenome representations use some kind of a graph as the basis for their approach

The sequence graphs used for this echo the representations used in Overlap-Layout-Consensus assembly algorithms (described in more detail in \textit{Genome Assembly} above), with the exception that pangenome graph representations typically disallow overlaps between different nodes.
The major reason for using this graph-centred approach have been the availability of a well-established file format and a sequence graph's flexibility, accomodating quite different approaches in a common software ecosystem\cite{paten17, matthews24, gfatools, garrison18}.

Early sequence-based pangenomes were constructed from VCF files obtained by calling variants between all samples and a common reference\cite{garrison18, pangenie}.
However, these representations inherited all of VCF's limitations, in particular

VCF's inability to represent nested or complex variants and the ambiguouos way structural variants are represented in VCF.
Additionally, VCF loses phasing information that cannot always be reconstructed during pangenome graph construction.

%Given that a representation is supposed to %ofor applications like read alignment or genome annotation would be cumbersome at best.
Fundamentally, VCF's list of differences approach was designed to store small variant genotypes across many samples for association studies, and is not suited to representing complete genomes.
More modern approaches directly construct their pangenome representation from the input sequences, avoiding VCF's limitations.

One may ask why the genomes need any kind of representation at all; storing the full sequence of all samples in separate (or even the same) FASTA file would preserve all information, and make obtaining the sequence trivial.
This approach is not without precedent; the BLAST database is maintained in a similar way\cite{blastdoc}.
While this »non-representation« would indeed not lose any information in the sample genomes, it would run into practical and conceptual limitations in most usecases.
On the practical side, representing all sequences separately takes a constant amount of space per sample.
This increases storage requirements, but more importantly makes using the reference in downstream analyses more memory- and compute-intensive.
For example, a resequencing pipeline would need to align every read to >400 haplotypes if it were to switch to the current human pangenome reference represented in this way.
%The current human pangenome reference's raw haplotypes  
This also shows the conceptual limitations of this non-representational approach; what would it mean in the described experiment for a read to align only to one genome, all 400, or to some but not others -- particularly if it does not align equally well all the time?
Treating all samples separately may not remove any feature of the samples, but it fails to represent the information gleaned from their aggregation.\\
In my view, this is still a useful thought experiment to compare proposed representations with the raw, uncollapsed haplotypes.%, particular when considering their use in downstream analyses.
This can highlight advantages of different proposed representations and elucidate limitations, ultimately revealing allowing an informed choice of representation and providing directions for the design of future implementation.\\%limitations and highlighting desired features of a representation.
In particular, two features emerge in this consideration.
First, the ability to reconstruct the input haplotype sequences from a representation; this property guarantees that the representation does not lose information present in the samples, and ensures that it can be converted to any other pangenome representation via the raw data.%(as the non-representation is the starting point for all attempts at representation).
%There are two major reasons why this may not be the case in a given representation
Reconstructing the input may be impossible either because the representation discards some genomic sequence from the input haplotypes, or because the representation is ambiguous as to the arrangement of the sequences -- i.e. keeps the pieces, but not the instruction manual for how to put them back together.
Pangenome representations fulfilling this criterion can be conceptualized as a compression or annotation of the input haplotypes.\\ % too wishy washy?
Second, a pangenome representation should be compact, scaling slower than the raw uncollapsed haplotypes.% and not require excessive space.
This serves to make downstream analyses computationally more feasible given the scale of recent datasets, and to avoid excessive redundancy that would otherwise make it difficult to draw conclusions from approaches using the representation.%in the outputs of such analyses.
In regards to this consideration, different representations can be thought of as occupying different places along a spectrum of redundant vs. collapsed representations\cite{paten17}.
While the input haplotypes occupy the extreme end of collapsing nothing and preserving all redundancy, an imaginary fully collapsed representation would consist only of the bases \texttt{A}, \texttt{C}, \texttt{G}, \texttt{T}, and arguably be even less useful in practice.\\
Different proposed approaches sit at different points along this spectrum, and it is helpful to bear this in mind when choosing between them and setting their parameters, particularly considering that different analyses may require or prefer different degrees of collapse \cite{hickey24, sierra24, igolkina25}

\subsection*{The Pangenome Graph Builder (PGGB)}
% pggb, vargraph
% go semi mathsy?
The Pangenome Graph Builder (PGGB) is the second prominent pangenome graph representation toolkit.
It was released initially as a shell script-based pipeline in 2021, then ported to a nextflow-based pipeline and published in 2024\cite{pggbv01, garrison24}.
It takes a different approch to minigraph, in that the genome representation is based on the concept of a variation graph.
A variation graph is a datastructure representing all pairwise alignments of a set of sequences in a graphical manner\cite{garrisonthesis}.
While initially formally introduced as the graphical representation of a non-deterministic automaton built from the sequences, I will here introduce it in terms of alignment graphs.
Consider the input sequences as segments of arbitrary but constant size
\footnote{Both overlapping and non-overlapping segments yield a similar alignment graph, if self-alignments are disallowed.}
. A bipartite graph can be constructed with the segments of two input samples as nodes, and an edge between two nodes iff they align to each other.
The segments of another sample can now be added, and the graph extended to a tripartite graph by connecting nodes in the previous two samples with aligning nodes in the new sample; this process can be iterated until the graph includes all samples.
A segment shared by all or a subset of samples will induce a clique in this graph, that is a fully connected subgraph.
A variation graph can be constructed by clique decomposition of this graph; to do this, consider a maximal set of cliques of the alignment dgraph described above and take this as the nodeset of a new graph.
Then project the paths implied by the sample genome's ordered segments into this path, and optionally collapse nodes where they do not diverge.
%A segment that is shared by all genomes will thus ind
%If we consider the sequences as 
%It can be viewed as a maximal clique decomposition of an alignment graph
\\
The approach taken by PGGB to construct a pangenome variation graph is not too different from this characterization of variation graphs:
PGGB first maps segment-based homology using an approximate minimizer-based approach.
A random subset of the sequences assigned as homologous is then aligned at basepair resolution.
Both of these steps are implemented in the WFmash package, combining the MapMash and WaveFront alignment algorithm\cite{marco-sola20, kille23}.
Subsequently, an alignment graph is constructed and transitive closure is applied to recover alignments missed by the subsampling performed in the previous step\cite{garrison22, garrison24}.
Fully connected components are collapsed into nodes in the variation graph-based pangenome graph.
This initial representation is postprocessed in multiple steps to remove inconsistencies in the all versus all pairwise alignments and redundant nodes.
In this postprocessing step, complex regions of the graph may be realigned using a graph-based multiple sequence alignment. 
The graph induction is implemented in the seqwish package, while postprocessing uses the SMOOTHXG and GFAfix tools\cite{garrison22, garrison24, gfafix}.\\
The PGGB pipeline is quite complex and involves multiple steps with $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ worst-case complexity, making it very compute intensive although the implementation of base-level alignment subsetting has decreased compute requirementssomewhat.
PGGB's nextflow implementation also scales more effectively on HPC systems, decreasing analysis latencies if sufficient computational resources are available.\\
%collapses repeats
As PGGB reports (potentially cyclic) paths for all samples, it is possible to reconstruct the input genomes from its representation.
Compared to minigraph's representations, PGGB's output graphs tend to be much more collapsed.
Though this depends on the exact parameters chosen during the all vs all alignment, in general PGGB collapses all repetitive elements, which can lead to many nonlocal edges in repeat-rich genomes\cite{cheng25}.\\
%In general, because its graph is constructed from local all versus all alignments, PGGB can collapse nonlocal sequences if they have similar sequence and contain nonlocal edges, particularly in species with structurally complex or highly repetitive genomes\cite{cheng25}.
PGGB graphs also frequently contain cycles, or multiple successive very small nodes that are not straightforward to untangle\cite{cheng25}. % better citation?

\subsection*{The Synteny and Rearrangement Identifier (SyRI)}
\begin{figure*}[ht]
	\centering
	\includesvg[width=0.8\textwidth]{img/syn.svg}\\[2\baselineskip]
	\includesvg[width=0.8\textwidth]{img/synsvs.svg}
	\caption{
		\textbf{Illustration of the SyRI algorithm.}\\
		\textbf{Top}, in the first step, SyRI identifies a syntenic backbone of conserved genome structure between the reference and query.
		\textbf{Bottom}, after identifying syntenic regions, leftover regions are known to be structurally variable, and are annotated appropriately in a multi-step process (see main text).
		Finding conserved structure first both makes the algorithmic problem tractable, and helps to avoid calling spurious similarities as structural variation.
		This is illustrated by the translocation drawn with a dashed outline (purple): here the large, syntenic alignments including both regions are a more parsimonious annotation than inserting a translocation based on the drawn secondary alignment.\\
		Figure inspired by \cite{goel19}.
	}
	\label{fig:syri}
\end{figure*}

SyRI is a tool written by Manish Goel to compare the genome structure between a reference and query assemblies\cite{goel19}.
Its core insight is that finding \emph{syntenic}, that is structurally conserved, segments is far easier than finding rearrangements, and after all syntenic regions have been found all that is left to do is to annotate the remaining sequence as the right kind of structural rearrangement.\\
This is reflected in the SyRI algorithm:
First, syntenic regions are identified from a whole-genome alignment by selecting a longest path of consistent alignments covering each chromosome.
Two alignments are considered consistent if the regions they align occur in the same order in both genomes -- intuitively, this can be understood as two lines drawn between the aligning regions not crossing each other (compare also Fig. \ref{fig:syri}).
Finding this longest syntenic path is a complex optimization problem;% it is harder than ordinary sequence alignment in particular, because a region can have multiple alignments 
SyRI approaches it by representing aligned blocks reported by ordinary whole-genome aligners as nodes in a graph.
It then finds a longest path in this graph through a divide-and-conquer algorithm, falling back to an exact solver implemented in the \texttt{longestrunsubsequence} or a greedy heuristic in the base case, depending on its size and complexity.% with an edge if they are adjacent and consistent 
Second, inverted alignments corresponding to gaps in the alignment are found, added to the syntenic path and annotated as non-translocated inversions.
After that, alignments in remaining gaps are annotated as translocations, if available.
Secondary inconsistent alignments of syntenic regions are likewise annotated as duplications.
In a final step, cross-chromosomal translocations are identified and regions without any alignment (syntenic or otherwise) are annotated as highly diverged.\\
After finding genome structure, SyRI goes back to the raw alignment to identify small variants between the reference and sample.
Both small and large variants are reported in a custom output format; small variants are also reported as a VCF file.
SyRI's output can be visualized using the plotsr utility\cite{goel22}.

Using SyRI, structurally conserved and variable regions between a sample and reference assembly can be found in a straightforward and efficient manner.
In my Bachelor's Thesis supervised by Manish Goel, I developed a tool to merge SyRI's synteny calls across multiple samples called against a common reference.
Later, I worked on extending this tool with a realignment step to recover structural features not present on the reference, and named it the multisynteny detector (msyd).
As msyd's structural similarity calls form the baseline for the synteny-centred graph representation of a pangenome described in this thesis, I will give an outline of msyd's core concepts.
For more details on the msyd algorithm, the interested reader is referred to my Bachelor's Thesis and msyd's documentation, available in the GitHub repository.

% core vs merasynteny
The central concept of msyd is that of multisynteny.
A multisyntenic region is defined as a set of at least two intervals on multiple, pairwise different samples such that any two intervals align to each other as part of SyRI's syntenic backbone when comparing the two samples.
We further require multisyntenic regions to be maximal, defined as requiring that neither the multisyntenic region itself nor any set of subintervals can be extended to larger intervals or additional samples without violating the mutual synteny requirement.
%that is there cannot be any other interval  that can be added to the multisyntenic region or a set constructed from subintervals of the multisyntenic region while still fulfilling the requirements given above.
While a definition without requiring maximality would be consistent, it would allow multiple multisynteny annotations of the same dataset as well as overlapping multisyntenic regions.
If a multisyntenic region encompasses all samples in a population/dataset, we term it to be core syntenic; otherwise we call it merasyntenic (from ancient Greek μερiκως, meaning partial).
% briefly, synteny intersection + realignment
Synteny is transitive; that is, if region $a$ on sample $A$ is syntenic to regions $b, c$ on samples $B, C$, $b$ also has to be syntenic to $c$, and vice versa, as synteny is always symmetric.
This allows the efficient identification of all core syntenic regions: if we choose any sample as a reference to compare all other samples to, we can intersect the synteny calls on the common reference sample to obtain all core synteny with only $\mathcal{O}(n)$ required whole-genome alignments and SyRI runs.
This will also identify any merasynteny that includes the chosen reference sample, but miss any for which this is not the case.\\
In order to recover these missing annotations, msyd includes a realignment algorithm that iteratively repeats the intersection procedure with different references until all multisynteny is found.
This is performed separately for each gap between two coresyntenic regions -- as they cover all samples, there cannot be a multisyntenic region that crosses a coresyntenic region --, avoiding expensive whole-genome alignments at this step, as it can require up to $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ alignments.
