Recent progress in genome sequencing and assembly technologies have made full genome assemblies more and more accessible, to the point where it is now feasible to obtain assemblies for entire sample populations in many species.
Translating this data into knowledge has, however, been a significant challenge so far.
Genome assemblies are a large and heterogeneous kind of data.
Making the information contained within them accessible is far from trivial, and requires computational approaches scaling to efficiently utilize large computational resources.

The matter of how to represent today's genome assembly datasets is absolutely crucial to any attempt at analyzing them, both in allowing efficient algorithms to be constructed and in limiting, enabling or focusing the questions that we ask a dataset.
Despite several recent proposals and a slowly maturing software ecosystem, I will argue that the representation of today's genome datasets is an unsolved question still requiring considerable conceptual contributions.

\section*{Historical Background to Modern Genomics}

The first recognition of the genome as a concept dates to Gregor Mendels work in peas.
He represented the genome of his peas as combinations of upper/lowercase letters indicating different alleles.
Given the lack of any genomic technologies, the genetic identity of a given pea could only be determined by its phenotype or the phenotypes of its offspring in a controlled cross.
Mendel's genomes also lacked any internal structure; gametes were viewed as produced from independently assorted alleles of the parental genotypes.
The genome as such was a passive container of alleles, and not conceptualized beyond this.\\
After the rediscovery of Mendel's work in the early 20th century, the discovery of chromosomes as the bearer of heredity information and further crossing experiments performed among others by Thomas Hunt Morgan's laboratory in \textit{Drosophila melanogaster} revealed the genome to consist of chromosomes with a linear structure.
Through careful crossing, chromosomes were mapped, again determining the genotype solely from the phenotype of the organism and its offspring.
These genetic maps charted genes with observable phenotypes along the chromosomes, and revealed a shuffling process known as recombination, acting by discrete crossover events with a determinable likelyhood at each position along the genetic map.\\
The genome had evolved from a few unordered letters to a set of linear maps, whose cellular component (if not molecular identity) was known to contemporary researchers.
Like Mendel, however, genes along the genome were thought of and named for their observable, segregating phenotypes, and the absence of any genomic technologies made genotyping a slow and labourious process.\\
Later, microscopy of chromosomes revealed a banded structure, which work in maize by Barbara McClintock and in \textit{Drosophila spp.} by Theodosius Dobzhansky showed to sometimes vary across individuals, initially without giving rise to observable macroscopic phenotypes.
The discovery of these so-called »cryptic polymorphisms« was the first time a genotype had been determined by other means than phenotypic observation.
Cytogenetics can thus be termed the first genomic technology; in fact, through careful observation of the band structure, these polymorphisms could be identified as inverted or copy-pasted segments along the chromosomes, hinting at the mechanism of their formation -- later giving rise to McClintock's discovery of transposable elements.\\
%Around the same time, the synthesis of Mendel's rediscovered works and contemporary evolutionary biology, as well as contacts with ecology led to the emergence of an early theory of population genetics, a first way to systematically think about the behaviour of genomic variation in a population.
%Applying this new population genetic theory to the observed allele frequency of inversions in captive \textit{D. pseudoobscura} populations, these »cryptic polymorphisms« were revealed to have an effect on observed fitness, in particular in heterozygotes, a phenomenon that was then termed heterosis.

Increasingly, the study of the genome became emancipated from the reliance of clearly segregating phenotypes for every genetic polymorphism, and the discovery of rearrangements revealed that genomes in a species could vary beyond the single-gene level. % a genetic or cytogenetic map could vary between different individuals of a population.
Genotyping technology allowed population genetic studies for the first time, though these were limited in the number of polymorphisms that could be studied at a time. %studied a limited amount of polymorphisms at a time.

The discovery first of DNA as the molecule carrying genetic information and later of its 3D structure in the 1940s and 1950s by Avery\cite{avery42} and Franklin, Crick and Watson\cite{watson53} provided for the first time a vision to describe the genome at a molecular level.
More molecular studies followed like the discovery of transcription, replication and the decoding of the genetic code, providing some understanding on a basepair level.
This opened the door to a radically different way of conceptualizing the genome -- not as an ordered map of segregating phenotypes or bands under a microscope, but as a string of bases along a phosphate backbone.
As DNA became accessible by cloning, PCR and eventually early sequencing methods, databases of DNA sequences started to emerge.
These would contain small fragments of the genome, often genes of particular interest, promotor or plasmid sequences.
The sequences would be stored as letters, but unlike Mendel's early use of letters to indicate alleles, the letters now indicated the bases \texttt{A}denine, \texttt{C}ytosine, \texttt{G}uanine and \texttt{T}hymine and were ordered by their position in the genome, typically starting from the $5'$ and ending at the $3'$ end. 
Early databases were still printed on paper and distributed by mail as books to university libraries, but they soon embraced early mainframe computers as they became available to universities.
Databases used a variety of file formats at the time -- often based on previous print conventions --, but the format used by the popular FASTP\cite{lipman85} and FASTA\cite{pearson88} programs came to dominate. %, but for nucleic acid databases the format supported by the fast-All programme, colloquially known as FASTA format, soon took the lead.
This format represents sequence in an \texttt{ACGT}-encoding with nonsignificant linebreaks to fit on early computer screens, along with header lines containing the sequence identifier to differentiate different sequences in the same file. 
Additional metadata can also be added to the header line, and gaps inserted indicating individual unknown bases or unresolved breaks in the sequence. %for each sequence containing the sequence name and additional metadata, if present. 
FASTA's format and variants based on it are still in common use today for a variety of purposes. 
In particular, it has become the de facto standard for representing reference genome assemblies after being chosen by major reference genome projects for distributing their references.%, and FASTQ format 

% whole genome assembly projects
Increasing throughput of Sanger sequencing opened up the possibility of obtaining the base sequence of entire genomes.
After early success in viral and bacterial genomes in the early 1990s, this culminated in large, well-funded global consortia preparing reference genomes for a number of eukaryotic species in the late 90s and early 00s, most notably \textit{A. thaliana}, mouse and human\cite{athal00, chinwalla02, lander01, venter01}.
% revive genetic map
The ambition to sequence not just individual genes of interest, but the entire genome can be viewed as a revival of Hunt Morgan's concept of a per-species genetic map.
The genome would not just be a scientific outcome in and of itself, but serve also as a community resource and to avoid duplicating sequencing and mapping efforts by individual groups.
A major component of specifically the Human Genome Project was also technology and standards development, along with outreach to the scientific community and general public.
Altogether, this wave of genome projects aimed to establish a new mode of thinking about genomes and more broadly biology, »ushering in the post-genomic era« in the language of the time\cite{perbal15, collins03}.
% gaps as novel concept, contiguity metrics

With the reference genome deciphered for some common model species, genomicists were now able to compare samples to the reference genome -- both conceptually, and by using the reference to develop cost-efficient microarrays.
This powered the HapMap project immediately after the human reference had been assembled, aided by the long linkage blocks and low overall variation in the human genome\cite{altshuler05}.
% short read sequencing useful + cheap
Later, sequencing costs declined sharply with the advent of early next-generation sequencing technologies.
Especially in the beginning, these produced very short and error-prone untargeted reads that were not directly useful on their own.
Using newly developed algorithms based on earlier approaches for searching for genes in reference genomes and databases\cite{li08, kent02}, the reads could be aligned to the reference genome, recovering the position they were sampled from.
Small differences in sequence between sample and reference could then be determined from these aligned reads using statistical models\cite{li08, mckenna10}.
A key advantage over microarrays was the ability for \textit{de novo} variant discovery and to avoid the need for labourious microarray design and manufacturing, particularly in non-model organisms.
At least for single-nucleotide variants or small indels in non-repetitive regions, next-generation sequencing allowed scalable comprehensive genotyping.
This drove a wave of projects sequencing ever larger samples, such as the 1000 Genome Project in human and the 1001 Genomes Project in \textit{A. thaliana}\cite{weigel09, durbin10}.
The motivation for these projects was increasingly shifting away from a technology development and community resource perspective towards association studies and medical applications (in humans).

% shift over to VCF format, 1000 GP/human genome diversity
In order to efficiently compute associations between genotype and phenotype, genotypes of multiple samples have to be aggregated into a single, numerical matrix representation\cite{shabalin12}.
To store the genotypes collected in large-scale sequencing efforts, the Variant Call Format was developed by the 1000 Genomes Project\cite{durbin10}.
VCF closely follows this matrix representation, and while updates over the years have added ways to encode larger variations into the standard, its representation remains centred on cataloguing single-nucleotide polymorphisms in multiple samples.\\
This represented a step away from genome assembly, going from the deciphering of the exact (if usually not complete) base sequence to a list of genotypes at segregating sites, analogous to Mendel's genotypes but at much higher scale and without requiring directly observable phenotypes.

\section*{Assembling Genomes}

In this section, I will give a brief introduction into the process used to assemble a genome, highlighting the current state of the art.
In particular, I will focus on long read-based \textit{de novo} phased assembly, as this offers the most complete characterization of the genome and has recently become economically possible in larger samples, including in species with fairly complex genomes.

% assembly now within reach
% go into assembly algos
As the genome is far too large to be sequenced in one go, it is fragmented and some of these fragments sequenced; these sequenced fragments are known as reads.
The problem of reconstructing the sequence of the entire genome from the sequence of the sampled reads is known as genome assembly, and is often compared to assembling a jigsaw puzzle from its pieces.
However, there are a few key differences between genome assembly and solving a puzzle; chiefly, sequencing reads vary in length and sometimes contain sequencing errors or artifacts of the biochemical processes required to prepare DNA for sequencing.
Also, unlike puzzle pieces, reads are typically sampled randomly from the genome, an approach known for historical reasons as shotgun sequencing.\\
We can already see two properties required for a sequencing dataset to be used to successfully assemble a genome; there need to be reads covering the entire genome and enough overlapping reads to correct any sequencing errors present in individual reads, lest they be propagated to the final genome assembly.
Both of these problems can be avoided by sampling more reads, so are in practice a minor problem in datasets of sufficient size -- but they still need to be accounted for during genome assembly.\\
A more stubborn problem is presented by repetitive or low-complexity regions.
These make reconstruction challenging by causing spurious similarity between reads, obscuring the real similarity signal -- in the puzzle analogy, this is analogous to the blue sky or dense foliage being the most challenging regions.\\
Longer reads can help resolve repetitive sequences in two ways.
First, a sufficiently long read can span the entire region containing the repeat, allowing direct reconstruction.
Second, most repetitive regions are not exact repeats; like other regions in the genome, they accumulate point mutations in the course of evolution.

However, seuqencing itself is not enough to obtain a genomic sequence; the genome assembly puzzle still needs to be solved.
Even for the smallest eukaryotic genomes, sequencing datasets are far too large for humans to process, making computer programs the mainstay for genome assembly from the very beginning, though human judgement is still often required for quality control, setting appropriate parameters and scaffolding.
Genome assembly is a complex computational problem
% footnote or main text?
\footnote{Most formulations fall within the complexity class of $\mathcal{NP}$-complete problems, with the notable exception of certain Eulerian path approaches that are polynomially bounded by the number of k-mers in a genome\cite{pop02, compeau11}.},
meaning that heuristic algorithms are required to make the problem tractable.
Early approaches used simple greedy heuristics joining maximally overlapping reads, but these often caused misjoins between different sequences containing the same transposable elements\cite{huang92, huang99, green96}.
Almost all later assembly softwares use one of two algorithmic paradigms, both based on a graph representation of the sequencing reads
\footnote{One recently developed linear-time algorithm presents a notable exception, see \cite{wong23}}.\\
De Bruijn-graph based assemblers split reads into overlapping fragments, termed $k$-mers.
These are represented in a graph as edges, connecting the two $k-1$-mers obtained by removing the first/last letter of the original $k$-mer\cite{pevzner01, compeau11}.
The genome has to contain all $k$-mers found in the reads; as such, it corresponds to a path traversing all edges in the graph, also known as an Eulerian path.
The Eulerian path in a graph can be found efficiently, but exact repeats of size greater than $k$ will be collapsed, and sequencing errors need to be identified and corrected prior to graph construction.
Most de Bruijn-graph based assemblers include additional heuristics to address these limitations.\\
The other approach is referred to as Overlap-Layout-Consensus (OLC)\cite{kececioglu95}.
In the graph representation used, entire reads are represented as nodes.
An edge is added between reads with overlapping sequence at their ends -- this is known as the overlap step.
This initial representation, also referred to as an overlap graph, is processed in a variety of ways, notably by compressing unbranching paths, removing transitive edges and reads entirely contained within another read.
From this simplified graph representation, known as a contig or assembly graph, the genome is reconstructed in the layout step by searching for an optimal path covering all nodes (equivalent to finding a linear layout of the graph, hence the name).
Finally, reads are aligned back to the path to correct sequencing errors in the final consensus step.
Particularly the layout step is a very hard algorithmic problem, requiring aggressive processing of the graph representation and heuristic approaches in order to be tractable.
Finding all read overlaps is also a computationally demanding step, although it can be parallelized efficiently.

Remarkably, both of these approaches have been adjusted to successive generations of sequencing technologies over the years, and neither has proven to definitely outperform the other as of now.\\
Currently, two assemblers are used for state of the art assembly.
Verkko is based on a de Bruijn graph approach, and hifiasm uses the OLC paradigm\cite{rautiainen23, cheng21}.
Both of these assemblers have similar specialized procedures integrating ONT ultralong reads to resolve difficult regions and improve assembly contiguity, as well as preprocessing steps performing read error correction\cite{cheng24, antipov25}.
To integrate ultralong reads, they are aligned to the initial unitig graph(hifiasm)/a compressed de Bruijn graph (verkko).
These alignments are then used to refine the graph, by adding additional edges (both) and duplicating nodes to resolve ambiguous paths (only hifiasm).

\subsection*{The Synteny and Rearrangement Identifier (SyRI)}

SyRI is a tool written by Manish Goel to compare the genome structure between a reference and query sequence\cite{goel19}.
Its core insight is that finding \emph{syntenic}, that is structurally conserved, segments is far easier than finding rearrangements, and after all syntenic regions have been found all that is left to do is to annotate the remaining sequence as the right type of structural rearrangement.\\
This is reflected in the SyRI algorithm:
First, syntenic regions are identified from a whole-genome alignment by selecting a longest path of consistent alignments covering each chromosome.
Two alignments are considered consistent if the regions they align occur in the same order in both genomes -- intuitively, this can be understood as two lines drawn between the aligning regions not crossing each other (compare also Fig. \ref{fig:syri}).
Second, inverted alignments are added to cover gaps in the path, finding inversions.
After that, remaining gaps are filled with inconsistent alignments annotated as translocations, if available.
Secondary inconsistent alignments of syntenic regions are annotated likewise as duplications.
In a final step, cross-chromosomal translocations are identified and regions without any alignment (syntenic or otherwise) are annotated as highly diverged.\\
After annotating genome structure, SyRI goes back to the raw alignment to identify small variants between the reference and sample.
Both small and large variants are reported in a custom output format; small variants are also reported as a VCF file.
SyRI's output can be visualized using the plotsr utility\cite{goel22}.

SyRI's syntenic calls are used as the base for msyd's identification of shared genomic structure (described below), so SyRI forms our most central dependency.
To maintain it in a usable state for msyd and more generally, SyRI was updated to use the 3.x version of \texttt{pandas}, and run in versions of python newer than 3.11.
This required a partial rewrite of SyRI's internal datastructures and the way they are accessed using Cython's TypedMemoryView interface to account for pandas' change to copy-on-write behaviour.
Along the way, some of SyRI's error handling was modernized, particularly to always give a non-zero return value on failure and avoid silent errors.\\
All changes made to SyRI were upstreamed, and incorporated into a new release.


\subsection*{The Multisynteny Detector (msyd)}

% how to balance with bsc thesis?
% maybe separate by section? this section only old stuff, new stuff in graph?
% more detail than syri, but try to focus on new things

Using SyRI, structurally conserved and variable regions between a sample and reference assembly can be found in a straightforward and efficient manner.
In my Bachelor's Thesis supervised by Manish Goel, I developed a tool to merge SyRI's synteny calls across multiple samples compared to a common reference.
Later, I worked on extending this tool with a realignment step to recover structural features not present on the reference, and named it the multisynteny detector (msyd).
As msyd's structural similarity calls form the baseline for the synteny-centred graph representation of a pangenome described in this thesis, I will give an outline of msyd's core concepts and the alterations made to it for this project in this section.
For more details on the msyd algorithm, the interested reader is referred to my Bachelor's Thesis and msyd's documentation, available in the GitHub repository.

% core vs merasynteny
The central concept of msyd is that of multisynteny.
A multisyntenic region is defined as a set of at least two intervals on multiple, pairwise different samples such that any two intervals align to each other as part of SyRI's syntenic backbone when comparing the two samples.
We further require multisyntenic regions to be maximal, defined as requiring that neither the multisyntenic region itself nor any set of subintervals can be extended to larger intervals or additional samples without violating the mutual synteny requirement.
%that is there cannot be any other interval  that can be added to the multisyntenic region or a set constructed from subintervals of the multisyntenic region while still fulfilling the requirements given above.
While a definition without requiring maximality would be consistent, it would allow multiple multisynteny annotations of the same dataset as well as overlapping multisyntenic regions.
If a multisyntenic region encompasses all samples in a population/dataset, we term it to be core syntenic; otherwise we call it merasyntenic (from ancient greek μερiκως, meaning partial).
% briefly, synteny intersection + realignment
Synteny is transitive; that is, if region $a$ on sample $A$ is syntenic to regions $b, c$ on samples $B, C$, $b$ also has to be syntenic to $c$, and vice versa, as synteny is also symmetric.
This allows the efficient identification of all core syntenic regions: if we choose any sample as a reference to compare all other samples to, we can intersect the synteny calls on the common reference sample to obtain all core synteny with only $\mathcal{O}(n)$ required whole-genome alignments and SyRI runs.
This will also identify any merasynteny that includes the chosen reference sample, but miss any for which this is not the case.\\
In order to recover these missing annotations, msyd includes a realignment procedure that iteratively repeats the intersection procedure with different references until all sequences are annotated.
This is performed separately for each gap between two coresyntenic regions\footnote{As they cover all samples, there cannot be a multisyntenic region that crosses a coresyntenic region}, avoiding expensive whole-genome alignments at this step.

% changes: parallelization, move away from pandas datastructures
In order to make msyd suitable for graph construction, the codebase was improved in a few key ways.
In particular, the core datastructures were rewritten to avoid SyRI-style pandas DataFrames and instead use a Cython list type per chromosome.
This new datastructure has a more convenient API, but more importantly can be efficiently serialized and thus allows parallelization across different chromosomes without interfering with Python's Global Interpreter Lock.
In total, this allows msyd to efficiently utilize more cores, especially for steps other than the sequence alignment.
