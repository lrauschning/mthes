To approach this problem, some clarity on what phasing is (and is not) is required, in particular how it relates to genome assembly.\\
We term a position at which a haplotype is different from all others present in the host organism a \emph{haplo-unique site}; they may correspond to single small variants, (parts of) larger variants, or clusters of multiple nearby variants that different haplotypes happen to have different alleles of.
A phasing of a genome can be defined formally as a function taking as arguments two different haplo-unique sites, and returning whether they are found on the same molecule of DNA or not, thus sharing or not sharing a haplotype
\footnote{Note that in this definition, different chromosomes are always considered separate haplotypes; this reflects the fact that chromosomes segregate independently in offspring, but an alternative definition of a haplotype by inheritance from the same parent may permit haplotypes covering more than one chromosome}
.
If we refer to the set of all haplo-unique sequences as $U$ and a partition of $U$ into haplotypes as $H := \{h_0, \dots, h_n\}$, this can be expressed formally as:
%such that $h_i \subset U$ and $\bigcup h_i = U$, this can be expressed formally as:
$$\mathcal{P}: U \times U \to \{0, 1\}$$
$$\mathcal{P}(u_0, u_1) := \begin{cases}1 & \textrm{if}\quad \exists h_i \in H: u_0, u_1 \in h_i \\ 0 & \textrm{otherwise} \end{cases}$$
In particular, $\mathcal{P(u_0, u_1} =0$ for all $u_0, u_1$ not on the same chromosome.
Defining phasing in terms of this boolean function rather than a haplotype partition directly has the advantage of being symmetric in regards to the arbitrary labeling of the haplotypes (i.e., $\mathcal{P}$ does not change if we decide to henceforth swap $h_1$ and $h_2$ or vice versa) and cleanly separating different chromosomes.
	\\
%How do we construct such a phasing?
Using this definition of phasing, we can proceed to examine the information we have available in a dataset of sequencing reads, and proceed to find criteria by which we can decide if a reliable phasing can, in principle, be constructed from it.
When encountering a read, we know all of its sequence to be derived from a single physical piece of DNA corresponding to a fragment of a single haplotype
\footnote{With the possible exception of chimeric reads as a result of PCR artefacts or other sequencing errors.}.
If two haplo-unique sequences $u_0, u_1$ are found on the same read $r := (u_0, u_1)$, we can assign them to the same haplotype; this construction can be transitively extended, if we have two reads $(u_0, u_1), (u_1, u_2)$, we can use the shared haplo-unique site $u_1$ to assign $u_0$ and $u_2$ to the same haplotype.
Note that, repetitive sequences are not inherently problematic for phasing; so long as the sequence contains sufficient haplo-unique sequences, it can be easy to phase (though possibly difficult to assemble); and vice versa, a perfectly nonrepetitive sequence may be difficult or impossible to phase if it contains large stretches shared by all haplotypes.

We can thus derive a simple minimal requirement for reconstructing the phasing of a genome, given in theorem \ref{theo-phase-simple}.

\begin{theorem}
	Given a set of reads $\mathcal{R}$ and a set of haplotypes $\mathcal{H}$, each modelled as consisting of an ordered set of haplo-unique sequences $\mathcal{H}_i := (u^i_0, u^i_1, \dots u^i_n)$, it is possible to construct a phasing $\mathcal{P}$ if and only if $\forall (u_j, u_{j+1}) \exists x \in \mathcal{R}: (u_j, u_{j+1}) \in x$, that is if every adjacent pair of haplo-unique sequences is covered by at least one read.

\label{theo-phase-simple}
\end{theorem}

A proof is simple to construct: if the condition is true, for any two haplo-unique sites on the same haplotype $u_i, u_j$ with $i < j$, a chain of neighbouring haplo-unique sequences $u_i, u_{i+1}, \dots, u_{j-1}, u_{j}$ can be found and by the transitive closure $\mathcal{P}(u_i, u_j)$ must equal $1$/true.
If we know all neighbouring haplo-unique sequences to be covered, the absence of such a chain implies that $u_i$ and $u_j$ do not share a haplotype, and thus $\mathcal{P}(u_i, u_j)$ must evaluate to $0$/false.\\

If the condition is violated for a pair of haplo-unique sequences $u_i, u_{i+1}$ on the same haplotype, we cannot resolve them directly; transitive closure does not help either, as they are by construction directly adjacent.\\
If all but one haplotypes have been successfully phased out at a location, it is in principle possible to infer the phasing of all non-assigned haplo-unique sequences to the missing haplotype, recovering even haplo-unique sites not fulfilling the requirement given in Theorem \ref{theo-phase-simple}.
This can be seen most clearly in the case of a haploid genome present in only a single copy; in this case, clearly no information at all is required for assigning phase, as there is \textit{a priori} only a single haplotype any sequence could be derived from.\\
However, considering practical limitations, having positive evidence for every haplotype may often be preferable; in this case, the condition in Theorem \ref{theo-phase-simple} is sharp.
Otherwise, it may be relaxed to require a read only for all except one of the haplotypes.
%requiring a read only a read covering all adjacent haplo-unique sequences on all haplotypes can be lowered to all except one of the haplotypes.

Using some statistical modelling, we can turn this simple minimal requirement into an estimate of the minimal sequencing coverage required to confidently resolve the phasing of a genome.
It should be noted that limitations in read accuracy and genome assembly and phasing algorithms may in practice impose somewhat higher requirements; however, calculating a lower bound can answer if single-technology phased assembly is at all possible, and provide guidance on which parameters are limiting factors and thus should prioritized for protocol optimization.

To ensure full phasing, the requirement of theorem \ref{theo-phase-simple} has to be met not just for every haplotype, but also for every position along the genome.
In particular, this means that the mean sequencing coverage will require accounting for the following requirements (illustrated in Fig. \ref{fig:covcalc}):
\begin{enumerate}
	\item every haplotype has to be sampled
	\item every position along the genome has to be sampled, and
	\item the read covering every position on every haplotype has to actually contain both adjacent haplo-unique sequences.
\end{enumerate}
We can model each of these requirements in turn, allowing us to derive an overall lower bound.

To model the first requirement, if we consider a given position along the genome, every read mapping there will be drawn from all possible haplotypes with equal probability.
Sampling is with replacement, if we assume our sequencing library to have sufficient size.
Asking how many samples need to be drawn to cover every haplotype with confidence is equivalent to the Coupon Collector Problem, for which multiple approximations have been derived in the literature. % find original citation, derive
For example, following the approach given below Lemma 3.8 in \cite{mitzenmacher17}, we can estimate that given an upper bound $\alpha$ of the probability of not having sampled all $n$ haplotypes, a sample of size at least $\lceil n(\log n - \log \alpha) \rceil$ is required.

The second requirement hearkens back to the early days of estimating sequencing requirements for whole-genome shotgun sequences -- except instead of ensuring every position is covered at least once, we need every position to be covered in sufficient depth to make sure the first requirement is met.
If we model observed coverage along the genome given a specified mean coverage using a Poisson distribution, we can derive a requirement for the mean coverage by solving for the quantile of the Poisson distribution.
This corresponds exactly to the inverse of the upper $\Gamma$ function, a numeric approximation of which is available in the \texttt{zipfR} package.

Finally, consider the effect of read length.
First, it can trivially be seen that reads larger than the gap between adjacent haplo-unique sites are required in order to construct a phasing.
%in order to construct a successful phasing, the read length has to be larger than the distance between adjacent haplo-unique sites; otherwise, 
But even reads of sufficient length may not be informative, if they happen to cover just one of the haplo-unique sequences.
We can compute the fraction of informative reads spanning both haplo-unique sequences for a given position from the fraction of valid starting positions, given that every read is uniquely specified by its starting position.
A starting position is valid if a read of length $\ell$ starting from it spans both haplo-unique sites; all valid starting positions therefore have to be before the first haplo-unique sites, and there will be $\ell-d$ many, with $d$ giving the distance between the two haplo-unique sequences and the length of each site assumed to be negligible compared to the read length.
As every read has a single starting position, a mean sequencing coverage of $X$ coverage corresponds to $X/\ell$ reads starting at every position along the genome in expectation.
We thus expect $(\ell-d)\frac{X}{\ell}=\frac{\ell-d}{\ell}X$ reads to fulfil this criterion.
As we assume reads to be Poisson distributed, we can simply multiply our coverage estimate derived in step 2 with this factor.
Note that read length only matters here in how it relates to the distance between two haplo-unique sites.
Therefore, $d$ can be fixed as $d:=1$, and adjusted read length considered by giving $\ell$ in units of $d$, formally $\hat\ell:=\frac{\ell}{d}$ .

% go into potato values
To provide some concrete minima for potato, a value for the size of regions common to different haplotypes is needed.
Two studies have investigated this in the haplotypes of a single phased potato assembly each\cite{sun22, xiao26}. 
They both report individual segments common to multiple haplotypes up to a megabase scale, but do not report exact length statistics or their frequencies;
an analysis using the Otava genome reported in ref. \cite{sun22} of 2619 shared segments larger than \qty{10}{\kilo\bp} revealed roughly 75\% of these to be below \qty{100}{\kilo\bp}, and over 90 \% to be smaller than \qty{200}{\kilo\bp} (Craig Dent, personal communication). 
From Table \ref{table:covcalc}, we can see that phasing through \qty{100}{\kilo\bp} regions using \qty{200}{\kilo\bp} reads while reaching a reliability score of 20 would require 64x coverage with ultralong reads. 
Relaxing the reliability score threshold to 10 (implying that 10\% of breaks are expected to be unresolvable), \qty{200}{\kilo\bp} regions could be phased with 54x coverage of \qty{300}{\kilo\bp} reads.\\
To summarize, the full phasing of a potato genome would require phasing across megabase scale haplotype-shared regions and is not achievable with current sequencing and library preparation techniques. 
However, the majority of shared segments are much smaller, and can feasibly be resolved using high-coverage ONT ultralong sequencing.\\
Using the distribution of common segments described above, I estimate that an assembly using 54x coverage of \qty{300}{\kilo\bp} reads would contain at least 200 breaks from large regions from regions larger than \qty{200}{\kilo\bp}, 50 from unphased regions between \qty{100}{\kilo\bp} and \qty{200}{\kilo\bp}, and additional 20 from shorter regions.
In total, this would imply an average of at least 6 phasing breaks per haplotype and chromosome.
For some applications, particularly in plant breeding, this may already be sufficient; for applications requiring complete phasing, additional information could be used to fully phase the assemblies.
While HiC data would be an obvious candidate, it might also be possible to use sample pedigrees or reference panels to resolve these breaks without additional sequencing.

Using msyd, it is possible to comprehensively characterize multisynteny in a dataset of genome assemblies.
Msyd reports its output in Population Synteny Format (PSF), a custom file format inspired by VCF
\footnote{Msyd also supports exporting to VCF format using symbolic allele annotations to store synteny calls alongside small variants, but symbolic alleles are not straightforward to parse and cannot represent all information present in msyd output, particularly alignments and non-reference multisyntenic regions.}.
A PSF record contains one mulitsyntenic region on one line, and starts with a BED-like position on the reference it has been aligned to, allowing tabix indexing.
Following this, the position the multisyntenic region each sample and the alignment to the genomic region of the reference is given, with each sample separated by a TAB character and samples the multisyntenic region is not present in annotated as \texttt{.}, analogous to how small variants are reported in VCF format.
\\
While this format is amenable to indexing and easy for users to parse in custom scripts, a significant downside to any custom file format is lacking integration with other tools.
In the last two years, version 1 of the GFA format and its rGFA subset, originally developed to store intermediary representations of genome assembly, has increasingly become the standard for representing pangenomic datasets, while its .
It is now supported by read-based genotypers, read and graph aligners as well as multiple visualization suites\cite{guarracino22, wick15}.
Both \texttt{vg} and \texttt{gfatools} provide utilities for the file format\cite{garrison18, gfatools}.\\
Given this developing software ecosystem and GFA's graph-based nature allowing greater flexibility than VCF format, finding a way to represent msyd's multisynteny calls in GFA1 format is desirable.
In this section, I outline how multisynteny can be represented as a graph, how this was implemented efficiently in the multisynteny detector, and derive some properties of this representation that make it attractive compared to other graphical representations of pangenomic datasets.
See the following section for how this representation can be used to define a pangenomic coordinate system, and the Discussion for its broader implications in the context of modern pangenomics and population genomics. % further development forward-looking implications

To see how to construct a graph representation of multisynteny, let us first consider how two multisyntenic regions $A, B$ can relate to each other.
In the following, we use $S(A)$ to refer to the set of samples $A$ is present on, and $A_i$ to refer to the interval representing $A$ in sample $i$.
$A_i$ is undefined if $i \notin S(A)$.\\
First, no two regions can overlap each other on any organism unless $A=B$, as any overlap of two regions containing different samples would imply the overlapping region to be multisyntenic between all samples in $S(A) \cup S(B)$, violating the maximality condition.
The same consideration makes it impossible for one multisyntenic region to contain another.
A situation that could give rise to such an overlap would instead be annotated by msyd as separate, adjacent multisyntenic regions. % for the, along with the non-overlapping regions $a \setminus b$ and $b \setminus a$. % . containing forbidden by the 
Knowing that multisyntenic regions cannot overlap, we can define directed sample-adjacency.
$A$ and $B$ are directedly $i$-adjacent, if there does not exist a multisyntenic region $C$ such that $A_i < C_i < B_i$.
To write $i$-adjacency, we use $A \prec_i B$.
Directed sample-adjacency is an asymmetric relation, and any multisyntenic region can only ever be predecessor and successor to at most one other region per sample.
We can further observe that it is impossible for $A$ and $B$ to »cross« each other, that is for the order of $A$ and $B$'s representative intervals to contradict each other on any two samples both $A$ and $B$ are present on, as this would violate the condition that both $A$ and $B$ be part of a syntenic whole-genome alignment.
Formally, this requirement can be expressed as $A_i < B_i \implies \forall j \in S(A) \cap S(B): A_j < B_j$.
We can use this property to derive a sample-independent definition of directed adjacency: $A \prec B \iff \exists i \in S(A) \cap S(B): \nexists C: A_i < C_i < B_i$.
This relation is similar to sample-adjacency, but is undefined if $A$ and $B$ do not share a sample.

Using our definition of adjacency, we can now define a multisynteny graph.
We first consider with the set of all multisyntenic regions as the node set, and connect two nodes with a directed edge if they are directedly adjacent.
The sequence assigned to the nodes corresponds to the sequence found at the sample used as reference during the identification of the multisyntenic regions; it could also be defined to be a consensus sequence derived from the alignments, though computing this would require an MSA, which may become prohibitively expensive as the number of samples grows larger.
To include private regions in our representation, we can substitute an adjacency edge with a significant gap with a node containing the gap's sequence, and connect the target/source nodes to the new node instead. % in between representing private sequences between two adjacent nodes containing a significant gap.
We call these private nodes, and other nodes multisynteny nodes, among which we can further differentiate core and merasyntenic nodes.\\
%As msyd's internal representation 
With this addition, the multisynteny graph becomes a pangenome graph; that is, for every sample there is an ordered set of nodes connected by directed edges such that the sequence obtained by walking along the path aligns well to the sample's genome, though small variants are not represented.
In fact, this ordered set is guaranteed to be an acyclic path, as any node is either a multisyntenic node and thus part of a syntenic alignment to another genome or a private node with only a single incoming and outgoing edge.\\%; in fact, we will be able to prove later that this graph is acyclic.\\
Reflecting that the addition of private regions allows the graph to represent the sequence of the pangenome along with multisynteny calles, we term it \emph{Multisyntenic Pangenome Graph (MPG)}.

				\eIf{dist(curr[\texttt{s}].multisyn[\texttt{s}], n.multisyn[\texttt{s}]) > $T$}{
					// $T$ is 0 by default, but can be configured\\
					make\_link(curr[s], \texttt{n})\\
				}{
					\texttt{p} := init\_node(\texttt{n}.multisyn[\texttt{s}] - curr[\texttt{s}].multisyn[\texttt{s}])\\
					make\_link(curr[\texttt{s}], \texttt{p})\\
					make\_link(\texttt{p}, \texttt{n})\\
					\textbf{yield} \texttt{p}\\
				}
					curr[s] := \texttt{n}\
			}
			\textbf{yield} \texttt{n}\\
		}
	}

	The default setting is $T=0$, meaning that even very small sequence between multisyntenic sequences are reported as private nodes.
	This maintains the invariant of not discarding any sequence during graph construction; if this is not a concern, setting it to e.g. $50$ can result in a more compact graph representation.\\
	The \texttt{make\_link} function stores a bidirectional link in the predecessor/successor lists of both nodes.\\
	The worst-case runtime of this algorithm is determined by the call to \texttt{.sort} in line 2; in practice the input received from msyd is for the most part presorted, and sorting can be ensured in linear time, making the average-case runtime $\mathcal{O}(|\text{get\_samples()}||\text{multisyns}|$.
	}

\end{algorithm}

Internally, the graph is represented as a sorted list of nodes, with each node containing an adjacency list of its predecessors and successors, labelled by the sample on which they are directedly adjacent.
This allows efficient forward and backward traversal in a simple datastructure. %, but avoids excessive pointer chasing and fragmented memory allocation.
Private nodes are encoded implicitly in the genomic coordinates associated with each multisyntenic node.
The graph is divided by chromosome, allowing all algorithms to be parallelized by chromosomes.\\
Since every node in the MPG has at most as many successors as there are samples, and the path of each sample through the graph is acyclic and mutually compatible, we can construct this graph representation using a simple linear scan through the multisyntenic regions.
During the scan, we store a reference to the node indicating the current position on each sample.
When encountering a new multisyntenic region, a new node is initialized containing predecessor-links to the current node on all samples the region contains.
The successor-lists of these nodes are then updated to contain the newly initialized node, and it is added to the node list.
This graph construction procedure avoids both lookahead and lookback by exploiting the nature of the MPG as union of a finite set of non-branching paths; to work successfully, it requires nodes to be scanned in an order compatible with the partial ordering derived from the directed adjacency relation.
This can be ensured by simply sorting the multisynteny calls according to this relation prior to scanning.\\
A sketch of the algorithm for computing an MPG from a multisynteny callset is given in Algorithm \ref{algo:mpg}.

The graph construction algorithm has expected and worst-case runtime complexity linear in the number of multisyntenic regions in the input and the number of samples.
In the worst-case, ensuring sorting could take longer than the actual graph construction; the total worst-case runtime complexity is given by $\mathcal{O}(n|MS| + |MS|\log|MS|)$, with $n$ as the number of samples and $|MS|$ as the number of multisyntenic regions in the input msyd callset.
The first term is the graph construction step, which involves up to $n$ steps for every multisyntenic region.\\
%which is linear in the number of nodes in the output; as every multisyntenic node cannot induce no more than $n$ private nodes, this gives a bound of $n|MS|$ nodes in the output graph.
%In the expectation, in a large sample most multisyntenic regions would not induce a private regions or at most a constant number of private regions, giving a bound of $\mathcal{O}(|MS|)$.\\
The second term comes from needing to ensure sorting of the multisyntenic regions prior to graph induction.
As msyd's intersection algorithm ensures sorting, only switches to different realignment targets can introduce breaks in this sorting.
This does not happen often in practice, so the appropriate output sorting is expected to take linear time in the average-case using an algorithm that can exploit prior sorted runs.\\%performed in expectation in linear time in expectation.
Memory-wise, the graph requires two references per sample and multisyntenic region beyond the base multisynteny representation, with only a constant single reference per sample additionally required during graph construction.

GFA export is performed by traversing the graph using the same order as during graph construction
\footnote{At no additional computational cost, as the sorting is retained in the node list.},
though in the worst case as many private regions as samples need to be annotated in every traversal step.
By default, the GFA format uses indexes introduced later in this section.
See that section for an an overview of the GFA export procedure in Algorithm \ref{algo:gfa}.\\
The GFA export supports using the W-line feature added in version 1.1 of the GFA standard to explicitly annotate all or a selection of sample's paths through the MPG. % , we can add the sample paths to the GFA output as W-lines.
Further, the origin and samples present in a node are added to each node using the annotations defined in the rGFA extension of the GFA1 standard.
%we can use node labels to annotate each node with its used reference, corresponding interval and all samples passing through it.
Annotating sample paths as W-lines requires one graph traversal per sample, although only the nodes contained in the sample need to be visited at this stage.\\

The GFA export step has the highest memory requirements of all graph construction steps, despite favourable computational complexity. 
This derives from the need to access genomes from many samples to obtain the node sequences in the graph. 
%In practice, this stage of the algorithm does however have the highest memory requirements, as the genomes of all organisms need to be available for annotating nodes in the GFA file with their respective sequences.
Using \texttt{pysam}'s FASTA class for dynamically reading from the genome files as required helped reduce required memory, but demand can still be substantial in datasets with many samples and large genomes. %though in the worst case all genomes are still required to be in memory simultaneously.

After having defined and introduced the multisyntenic pangenome graph, we can now investigate some of its properties in more detail.
First, we can see that the MPG is acyclic.
Again consider two nodes in the graph $A, B$.
Without loss of generality, we can assume both to be multisyntenic nodes, as any private node can only connect two multisyntenic nodes.
Let $a, b$ be the samples used as a reference when aligning $A, B$, and $S(X)$ and $X_i$ describe the samples $X \in \{A, B\}$ is present on and its representative interval on sample $i$, respectively. %hen we can refer to the samples used as a reference for $A, B$ as $a, b$
This time, we will also be using the syntenic alignment $\aln_{i\to j}$of two sample genomes $i, j$.
We will treat the alignment as a function mapping an interval on sample $i$ to the corresponding position on sample $j$.
If the input interval does not have a syntenic alignment anywhere on sample $j$, the alignment shall map it to a zero-width interval immediately after the last interval that aligns.
Note that $\aln$ is a monotonic function though not necessarily injective or surjective.
The definition of multisynteny requires that $\aln_{i\to j}(A_i) = A_j$ for all samples $i, j \in S(A)$ for a multisyntenic region $A$.\\
We assume $A$ and $B$ to induce a cycle, that is for there to be both a path $A\to C\to\dots\to B$ and $B\to\dots\to C'\to A$.
Private nodes along this path can be ignored, as they are essentially extended edges between multisyntenic nodes.
By the construction of the multisynteny graph, these paths imply that $A\prec C\prec\dots\prec B\prec \dots \prec C'\prec A$.
For every one of these steps, there must be at least one sample $\ell_i$ such that $X^i_{\ell_i} < X^{i+1}_{\ell_{i+1}}$, with $X^i$ as the multisyntenic region at position $i$ along the path.
Since alignments are monotonic, and intervals of multisyntenic regions have to align to each other for all samples the region is present in, we can write each these steps in terms of an alignment to any sample.
We further know that both $\ell_0, \ell_i \in S(A)$.
We write
\begin{eqnarray*}
A_i < X^0_i < \aln_{\ell_i \to i}(X^1) <\dots < \aln_{\ell_{i} \to i} (X^{i}) < \aln_{\ell_i \to i} (A_{\ell_i})\\
A_i < \dots < \aln_{\ell_i \to i} (A_{\ell_i})\\
A_i < A_i
\end{eqnarray*}
, in the last step using that all regions in $A$ have to align to each other.
This is obviously a contradiction, proving that the graph is acyclic.

This fact allows us to further characterize the MPG.
As every path has to pass through coresyntenic nodes exactly once and the graph is acyclic, the coresyntenic nodes induce regular bottlenecks in the graph, giving it a linear backbone.
Subgraphs between two coresyntenic regions form (super-)bubbles, as the only ingress/egress nodes are the coresyntenic regions itself.
This guarantees that there are no non-local edges in the MPG.\\
Nodes always have equal in- and outdegree, except for those at contig breaks or telomere ends in T2T assemblies.
The degree of a node is also bounded by the number of samples passing through it; as such, no node can have more adjacent edges than twice the number of samples.
As the start/end of each node corresponds to the start/end of a syntenic alignment, all non-private nodes with less than two successors represent deletions.\\
This bound on the number of edges is a useful property, as many classic graph algorithms scale linearly in the number of edges.

To navigate a genome representation for any use, a system allowing for positions to be specified and compared is required.
In a linear reference genome, a chromosome ID and number corresponding to the number of bases from the 3' end is sufficient for this purpose.
This is a powerful and intuitive index: it supports both qualitative and numeric comparisons -- intuitively, the expressions $a < b$ and $|a - b|$ when comparing two positions $a,b$ --, and can seamlessly be extended to describe intervals as well as individual positions.\\
In designing a navigation aid for a graph-based representation of multiple genomes, it would be preferable to keep as many of these features as possible while remaining simple to understand and efficient to implement.
In this subsection, I will introduce Pangenomic Coordinates (PanCos), an addressing system exploiting the Multisyntenic Pangenome Graph's structural guarantees to provide an interpretable, straightforward way to specify and compare positions in all or some of the genomes simultaneously.

A PanCo consists of two natural numbers, and uniquely identifies a node in the MPG when combined with a chromosome ID.
Positions within the node can be given analogous to a linear reference, as a natural number indicating the distance from the 3' end on the sample used as reference for the MPG node.
Using the alignments that induced the multisynteny, this can be mapped to syntenic positions in all samples present in the multisyntenic region.
PanCos are typically written in the form \texttt{27.3} or \texttt{17.0} when working within a chromosome, or as \texttt{Chr04:27.3} otherwise.
Alternatively, \texttt{\#} as a separator, to provide compatibility with the HPRC's Pangenome Sequence Naming convention
\footnote{Some gfa tools require nodes to have numeric IDs; in this case, \texttt{000} may be used as a separator that will maintain unique IDs in typical cases.}\cite{pansn}.
For easier human readability, a PanCo is prefixed by msyd with the letters \texttt{MERA}, \texttt{CORE} or \texttt{PRIV} dependending on its status when writing output files.

The first number of the PanCo indicates the preceding coresyntenic node; if there is none, it is set to 0.
This can only happen to multisynteny found between the start of a chromosome and the first multisyntenic regions, often representing structural diversity in subtelomeric repeat arrays.
As coresyntenic nodes have a total ordering and induce bottlenecks in the graph, the first number can be used to compare PanCos.
That is, given two PanCos $\texttt{A.a}, \texttt{B.b}$ and $\texttt{A} > \texttt{B}$, $\texttt{A.a} > \texttt{B.b}$ follows regardless of the values of $\texttt{a}$ and $\texttt{b}$.
The second number indexes merasyntenic nodes found between two core syntenic nodes.
As the MPG is acyclic, its directed edges imply a partial ordering.
The second number's are assigned to be consistent with this partial ordering; that is, $\texttt{A}=\texttt{B}$ and $\texttt{a} > \texttt{b}$ implies $\texttt{A.a} \nless \texttt{B.b}$, meaning that either $\texttt{A.a} > \texttt{B.b}$ on all shared samples, or the two do not share any samples and cannot be compared by transitive closure of intermediary nodes, either.
%Bearing in mind that no direct comparison is possible between nodes in some subgraph configurations, these are the strongest guarantees available.
As the preceding coresyntenic node covers all samples occurs before any merasyn, , its second number is assigned as $0$; it follows that $\texttt{A.0} < \texttt{A.x}$ for $x > 0$. % nodetopological ordering begins at the preceding coresyntenic node, 
In general, coresyntenic PanCos always end in $0$, and merasyntenic or private PanCos always have a non-zero number at the second position (\texttt{0.0} is undefined to avoid ambiguity).

Computing PanCos for coresyntenic regions is trivial, and can be done in linear time.
For merasyntenic regions, an asymptotically efficient way to obtain a valid ordering would be to compute a topological ordering of the subgraph between the preceding and following coresyntenic node, e.g. by depth-based traversal.
This has linear runtime in the number of nodes and edges in the subgraph; as the number of edges per node is bounded by the number of samples, the overall runtime is $\mathcal{O}(n|V|)$, with $n$ as the number of samples and $|V|$ as the number of merasyntenic nodes in the subgraph.
However, in practice a consistent ordering can be obtained much faster and without even using the graph structure, simply by sorting multisyntenic regions using a simple comparator: $A<B$ iff $\exists i \in S(A) \cap S(B): A_i < B_i$, with $A<B$ undefined if $A$ and $B$ do not share a sample.
It was shown earlier in this section that this ordering has to be consistent across all samples; it is in fact identical to the transitive closure of the directed adjacency defined as the MPG was introduced, and is already computed along with the graph representation.
All that is left to do, then, is to scan through all nodes and increment two counters for core and merasynteny, resetting the latter counter when incrementing the first.

\section*{Dataset Preparation}

To build a multisyntenic pangenome graph, a multisynteny callset is required.
To compute this callset, msyd requires a synteny callset of every sample against a common reference..
This can be computed with SyRI, and in turn requires a whole-genome alignment to the reference.
Msyd also requires these alignment files in order to retrieve the alignments of the multisyntenic regions.
For both realignment and graph construction, the genomic sequence of all samples is also required.
Msyd takes these inputs in samplesheet format, with one line per sample containing all files for that sample.

% describe nextflow pipeline
In order to generate the inputs required for msyd at scale in large datasets, a nextflow-based pipeline was implemented to perform alignments and SyRI runs, along with a script to automatically generate msyd's samplesheet from the pipeline output (see Appendix \ref{app:dataset}).
These preprocessing steps are typically much more computationally demanding than the msyd run itself; implementing them as a nextflow pipeline allows efficient parallelization across multiple nodes of a HPC environment\footnote{And provides robustness to jobs being cancelled when other jobs take more than their allocated memory, a persistent problem on the MPIPZ cluster}.

%\subsection*{Dependency Modernizations}
%In order 

% changes: parallelization, move away from pandas datastructures
Given the aim to make the pipeline scale to today's large pangenomic datasets, several improvements were made to the implementation of msyd's core functionality, reflecting both usability and performance improvements, including improved multithreading.
In particular, the core datastructures were rewritten to avoid SyRI-style monolithic pandas DataFrames and instead use a Cython list type per chromosome.
This new datastructure has a more convenient API, but more importantly can be efficiently serialized and thus allows parallelization across different chromosomes without the interference of Python's Global Interpreter Lock. % causing contentions between different worker threads.
In total, this allows msyd to use multiple cores, especially for steps other than the sequence alignment.

To maintain it in a usable state for the pipeline as well as general use, SyRI was updated to be compatible with the 3.x version of \texttt{pandas}, and run in versions of python newer than 3.11.
This required a partial rewrite of SyRI's internal datastructures and the way Cython's TypedMemoryView interface is used to account for pandas' change to copy-on-write behaviour in version 3.
Along the way, some of SyRI's error handling was modernized, particularly to avoid silent errors and always return a non-zero exit code in any error state.\\
All changes made to SyRI were upstreamed, and will be incorporated into the 1.8 release.

For software development and debugging, a previously prepared dataset based on the high-quality genome assemblies of the AMPRIL population described in refs. \cite{jiao20, ampril} was used.
Two datasets were prepared using the pipeline described above, one from human and one from potato genomes.

The human dataset was prepared from the HPRC's Phase 2 data release of >200 chromosome-scale phased assemblies, downloaded in Novembre 2025\cite{hprc2}.
This presents both a large dataset to test the graph's performance, and allows comparison to other pangenome graph representations, which were developed and primarily tested on human assemblies. 
As some assemblies had chromosome breaks, they were first scaffolded to the CHM13 T2T reference using RagTag\cite{ragtag}.
After that, the pipeline described above was run for all samples, using minimap2's \texttt{asm10} preset.

The potato dataset was prepared from the European potato pangenome reported in ref. \cite{sun25}.
It contains 10 haplotype-resolved assemblies for different cultivars for a total of 40 distinct haplotypes.
Maximally representative cultivars have been selected for assembly based on pedigrees from historical breeding records and short-read genotyping.
Altogether, the dataset is estimated to cover 85\% of the haplotype space present in modern European potatoes not contributed by recent introgression breeding.
The potato dataset both reflects the lab's research interest, and provides a stress-test of the multisyntenic pangenome graph:
potato genomes are very challenging to work with due to their high repeat content, extensive structural and small-scale diversity, and a breeding history shaped by ancient and modern introgressions.

When testing the pipeline, msyd's realignment step fell out as being often unreliable, frequently not reporting any new synteny during a realignment step.
Manual inspection revealed several cases where clear, segregating haplotypes were missed, particularly in short gaps.
Further testing indicated that minimap2 did not report any alignment in the analyzed regions, likely as a result of not finding enough minimizers.
While a MPG can still be constructed from a non-realigned dataset, it will represent non-reference sequences as private even though they may be shared.
