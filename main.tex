In this section, we want to obtain minimal input requirements for reconstructing phased potato genomes, and look at how different properties of our sequencing technology and genome characteristics affect these requirements.
To approach this problem, some clarity on what phasing is and is not is required, in particular how it relates to genome assembly.
In the cells of a given biological organism, there are often multiple copies of the genome – depending on species and tissue, 10 or more copies have been reported\cite{healey24}.
Because of standing variation or spontaneous mutational processes, these so-called haplotypes may differ between another in minor or major ways.
Variation on the same haplotype is inherited together, unless recombination breaks it up; this has given haplotypes the moniker »the unit of heredity«.
We term a position at which a haplotype is different from all others present in the host organism a \emph{haplo-unique site}; they may correspond to single small variants, (parts of) larger variants, or clusters of multiple nearby variants.
A phasing of a genome can be defined formally as a function taking as arguments two different haplo-unique sites, and returning whether they are found on the same molecule of DNA or not, thus sharing or not sharing a haplotype
\footnote{Note that in this definition, different chromosomes are always considered separate haplotypes; this reflects the fact that chromosomes segregate independently in offspring, but an alternative definition of a haplotype by inheritance from the same parent may permit haplotypes covering more than one chromosome}
.
If we refer to the set of all haplo-unique sequences as $U$ and a partition of $U$ into haplotypes as $H := \{h_0, \dots, h_n\}$, this can be expressed formally as:
%such that $h_i \subset U$ and $\bigcup h_i = U$, this can be expressed formally as:
$$\mathcal{P}: U \times U \to \{0, 1\}$$
$$\mathcal{P}(u_0, u_1) := \begin{cases}1 & \textrm{if}\quad \exists h_i \in H: u_0, u_1 \in h_i \\ 0 & \textrm{otherwise} \end{cases}$$
	\\
%How do we construct such a phasing?
We will concern ourselves with the minimal requirements for constructing a phasing from a set of long genomic sequencing reads, but it should be noted that HiC sequencing, a mapping population or parental or gamete sequencing may also serve to accomplish this goal.%, though less economically.
% justify why only deriving minimal estimates/can assemblers reach this?
Given that it is nowadays practical to assemble a complex plant genome using only Oxford Nanopore long read sequencing technology, our motivation is to answer the question if this extends to phased assembly, which has previously still required one or multiple of the aforementioned additional inputs.
Using only a single technology simplifies experimental planning and reduces both labour input and possible failure modes, allowing higher throughput and shorter timelines.

When encountering a read, we know all of its sequence to be derived from a single physical piece of DNA corresponding to a fragment of a single haplotype
\footnote{with the possible exception of PCR artefacts if the library was not prepared by a PCR-free protocol}.
If two haplo-unique sequences $u_0, u_1$ are found on the same read $r := (u_0, u_1)$, we can assign them to the same haplotype; this construction can be transitively extended, if we have two reads $(u_0, u_1), (u_1, u_2)$, we can use the shared haplo-unique site $u_1$ to assign $u_0$ and $u_2$ to the same haplotype.
Note that, contrary to genome assembly, repetitive sequences are not inherently problematic; so long as the sequence contains sufficient haplo-unique sequences, it can be easy to phase but difficult to assemble; and vice versa, a perfectly nonrepetitive sequence may be difficult or impossible to phase but easy to assemble if it contains large stretches shared by all haplotypes.

We can thus derive a simple minimal requirement for reconstructing the phasing of a genome, given in theorem \ref{theo-phase-simple}

\begin{theorem}
	Given a set of reads $\mathcal{R}$ and a set of haplotypes $\mathcal{H}$, each modelled as consisting of an ordered set of haplo-unique sequences $\mathcal{H}_i := (u^i_0, u^i_1, \dots u^i_n)$, it is possible to construct a phasing $\mathcal{P}$ if and only if $\forall (u_j, u_{j+1}) \exists x \in \mathcal{R}: (u_j, u_{j+1}) \in x$, that is if every adjacent pair of haplo-unique sequences is covered by at least one read.

\label{theo-phase-simple}
\end{theorem}

A proof is simple to construct: if the condition is true, for any two haplo-unique sites on the same haplotype $u_i, u_j$ with $i < j$, a chain of neighbouring haplo-unique sequences $u_i, u_{i+1}, \dots, u_{j-1}, u_{j}$ can be found and by the transitive closure $\mathcal{P}(u_i, u_j)$ must equal $1$/true.
If we know all neighbouring haplo-unique sequences to be covered, the absence of such a chain implies that $u_i$ and $u_j$ do not share a haplotype, and thus $\mathcal{P}(u_i, u_j)$ must evaluate to $0$/false.\\

If the condition is violated for a pair of haplo-unique sequences $u_i, u_{i+1}$ on the same haplotype, we cannot resolve them directly; transitive closure does not help either, as they are by construction directly adjacent.\\
If all but one haplotypes have been successfully phased out at a location, it is in principle possible to infer the phasing of all non-assigned haplo-unique sequences to the missing haplotype, recovering even haplo-unique sites not fulfilling the requirement given in Theorem \ref{theo-phase-simple}.
This can be seen most clearly in the case of a haploid genome present in only a single copy; in this case, clearly no information at all is required for assigning phase, as there is \textit{a priori} only a single haplotype any sequence could be derived from.\\
However, considering practical limitations, having positive evidence for every haplotype may often be preferable; in this case, the condition in Theorem \ref{theo-phase-simple} is sharp.
Otherwise, it may be relaxed to require a read only for all except one of the haplotypes.

Using some statistical modelling, we can turn this simple minimal requirement into an estimate of the minimal sequencing coverage required to confidently resolve the phasing of a genome.
It should be noted that limitations in read accuracy and genome assembly and phasing algorithms may in practice impose somewhat higher requirements; however, calculating a lower bound can answer if single-technology phased assembly is at all possible, and provide guidance on which parameters are limiting factors and thus should prioritized for protocol optimization.

To ensure full phasing, the requirement of theorem \ref{theo-phase-simple} has to be met not just for every haplotype, but also for every position along the genome.
In particular, this means that the mean sequencing coverage will require accounting for the following requirements (illustrated in Fig. \ref{fig:covcalc}):
\begin{enumerate}
	\item every haplotype has to be sampled
	\item every position along the genome has to be sampled, and
	\item the read covering every position on every haplotype has to actually contain both adjacent haplo-unique sequences.
\end{enumerate}
We can model each of these requirements in turn, allowing us to derive an overall lower bound.

To model the first requirement, if we consider a given position along the genome, every read mapping there will be drawn from all possible haplotypes with equal probability.
Sampling is with replacement, if we assume our sequencing library to have sufficient size.
Asking how many samples need to be drawn to cover every haplotype with confidence is equivalent to the Coupon Collector Problem, for which multiple approximations have been derived in the literature. % find original citation, derive
For example, following the approach given below Lemma 3.8 in \cite{mitzenmacher17}, we can estimate that given an upper bound $\alpha$ of the probability of not having sampled all $n$ haplotypes, a sample of size at least $\lceil n(\log n - \log \alpha) \rceil$ is required.
%TODO sharper estimate? give full derivation?

The second requirement hearkens back to the early days of estimating sequencing requirements for whole-genome shotgun sequences -- except instead of ensuring every position is covered at least once, we need every position to be covered in sufficient depth to make sure the first requirement is met.
If we model observed coverage along the genome given a specified mean coverage using a Poisson distribution, we can derive a requirement for the mean coverage by solving for the quantile of the Poisson distribution.
This corresponds exactly to the inverse of the upper $\Gamma$ function, a numeric approximation of which is available in the \texttt{zipfR} package.

Finally, not all reads covering a certain position may be informative, if they happen to cover just one of the haplo-unique sequences.
We can compute the fraction of informative reads spanning both haplo-unique sequences from the fraction of valid starting positions, given that every read is uniquely specified by its starting position.
A starting position is valid if a read of length $\ell$ starting from it spans both haplo-unique sites; all valid starting positions therefore have to be before the first haplo-unique sites, and there will be $\ell-d$ many, with $d$ giving the distance between the two haplo-unique sequences and the length of each site assumed to be negligible compared to the read length.
As every read has a single starting position, a mean sequencing coverage of $X$ coverage corresponds to $X/\ell$ reads starting at every position along the genome in expectation.
We thus expect $(\ell-d)\frac{X}{\ell}=\frac{\ell-d}{\ell}X$ reads to fulfil this criterion.
As we assume reads to be Poisson distributed, we can simply multiply our coverage estimate derived in step 2 with this factor.

\subsection*{Proof of Non-Circularity}
\newcommand{\aln}{\mathcal{A}}

We consider two multisyntenic regions $M$ and $M'$ that are both upstream and downstream of each other in genomes $q$ and $q'$, forming a circle in the graph representation.
Because downstream/upstreamness is transitive, this case covers all possible circles in the graph.
Formally, we can express this as $\exists q, q': M[q] < M'[q] \wedge M'[q'] < M[q']$.
Consider the alignment $\aln_{q \to q'}$.
If this hasn't explicitly been constructed, we can induce it from two alignments to a common reference $r$: $\forall i \aln_{q \to q'}:= \aln^{-1}_{q' \to r}[\aln_{q \to r}[i]]$.
Such an $r$ must exist, and be either the reference used during the realignment leading to $M$ or $M'$.
We can quickly see the two parts of the statement leading to a contradiction when we consider that multisyntenic regions are defined to be aligning on all organisms they cover:% at $\aln_{q \to q'}$ , we can see a contradiction emerge quickly to our :

\begin{eqnarray*}
M[q] &< M'[q] &\\
\aln_{q\to q'}[M[q]] &< \aln_{q \to q'}[M'[q]] &\mathrm{Alignments are monotonic}\\
M[q'] &< M'[q'] &\mathrm{but} M'[q'] < M'[q]\mathrm{!}
\end{eqnarray*}

%There are a few assumptions underlying this proof, some of which may be violated in practice.
Note that msyd's implementation of the realignment step uses \texttt{minimap2} to compute alignments.
Its fast minimizer-based approximative algorithm is not guaranteed to find the optimal alignment and may not always be monotonic, particularly in highly diverged or repetitive regions. % suboptimal alignments.% these conditions.
%An additional source of alignment ambiguity is the stitching together of leftover non-multisyntenic sequence during the realignment step.\\
The following SyRI step is also heuristic, but should be guaranteed to return a set of monotonic syntenic alignments, upholding the conditions for the above argument despite two heuristic steps.
If either of the steps misses or wrongly filters a genuine alignment as suboptimal, however, the representation may be under-collapsed, falsely reporting haplotypes as distinct when they are not.
